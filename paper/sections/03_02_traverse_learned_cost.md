### 3.2 Curiosity's traverse and the learned terrain price

**Traverse on the grid (E2).** PLACES poses [@PLACES] are converted to cells of the base grid. Consecutive poses (median 0.92 m apart) are joined by lines sampled every 0.25 m, which gives an 8-connected cell sequence of 4,687 steps over 4,477 visited cells. A terrain-only logistic baseline compares visited cells with unvisited cells at 2–50 m from the route, using slope, roughness, TPI and the mean slope in 5 and 15 m windows. It reaches AUC 0.526 (block range 0.473–0.612) in the 50 m corridor and 0.730 against the whole box. Terrain explains the choice of valley but not the path within it; in the boxwork sols it is at chance (0.47–0.50).

**Learned price (E1).** Maximum-entropy inverse reinforcement learning [@Ziebart2008] fits non-negative prices on the same features, conditioned on each drive's start and goal, with soft value iteration and an exact gradient. On synthetic terrain it recovers hidden prices (for example 2.514 ± 0.169 for a true 2.5). On Curiosity's route, at every scale tested (1 m and 5 m cells, pieces of 100–1000 m), the DEM features get a price of zero or close to zero, and the terrain model fits no better than distance alone (NLL per metre 1.17997 against 1.18002 for one demonstration per sol; held out, 1.18373 against 1.18349). With E8's sand, a small sand price holds out of sample: held-out NLL per metre 1.18281 against 1.18349 for distance alone, better in 4 of 5 blocks. It becomes a terrain factor of 1.0–1.115 on E3's energy, the "human cost". Using a price learned from a rover's drivers as a relative price for an astronaut is an assumption. A boulder price from E9 did not hold out of sample (negative result).
