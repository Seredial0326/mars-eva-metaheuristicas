# Curiosity in the Gale crater boxwork (Altadena member, Mount Sharp), sols ~4534-4848 (May 2025 - Mar 2026): ridges vs hollows, drill targets, science value

Status note: As of Nov 2026 no peer-reviewed paper on the in-situ boxwork results was found. Everything about in-situ mineralogy comes from **LPSC 2026 abstracts (preliminary, not peer-reviewed)**, JPL press releases and mission blog posts. The only peer-reviewed boxwork source found is the orbital study Siebach & Grotzinger (2014, JGR Planets).

Campaign timeline (from the deputy project scientist's blog): Phase 1 "first approach" sols 4534-4570 (May-Jun 2025, Altadena drill); Phase 2 "regional context" sols 4571-4599; Phase 3 "best-expressed boxwork" sols 4600-4805 (Jul 2025 - Feb 2026, Valle de la Luna + Nevado Sajama drills); Phase 4 "final look" sol 4805 onward, including the contacts with neighbouring units to the east and south — [NASA blog, Sols 4804-4811 (Fraeman, 17 Feb 2026)](https://science.nasa.gov/blog/curiosity-blog-sols-4804-4811-kicking-off-the-final-phase-of-boxwork-exploration/). Curiosity entered the boxwork unit on sol 4534 (8 May 2025) — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf).

## 1. What did Curiosity find on ridges vs hollows? (Checking the assumption "ridges = clays, hollows = carbonates")

### Takeaway
The team's assumption is **broadly correct but too simple**. Drilled samples, one per setting, show the ridge (Nevado Sajama) **enriched in phyllosilicates (clays) plus ferric oxy(hydr)oxides (hematite, goethite) and some Ca-sulfate**, and the hollow (Valle de la Luna) holding **abundant siderite (Fe-carbonate), no phyllosilicates, almost no hematite/goethite/anhydrite, and more pyroxene**. But **bulk chemistry (APXS, ChemCam) shows no clear, consistent difference** between ridges and hollows. Clays are also not unique to ridges: the "background" Altadena sample, from outside the ridge/hollow pairs, also has clays. Ca-sulfate veins and nodules occur throughout, and the dark ridge centrelines turned out to be **sand-filled open fractures with no visible mineral fill**.

### Cited Findings
**Mineralogy (CheMin XRD; preliminary)**
- Three drill samples were measured by CheMin: Altadena (AD, a "background" sample taken just after entering the boxwork unit), Valle de la Luna (VL, bedrock in a hollow) and Nevado Sajama (NS, "typical ridge material") — [Hausrath et al., LPSC 2026 #1489](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1489.pdf)
- All three samples are about 50% X-ray amorphous material and about 20% plagioclase — [Hausrath et al., LPSC 2026 #1489](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1489.pdf)
- Compared with AD and NS, the hollow sample VL has "abundant siderite; nearly no ferric oxy(hydr)oxide (hematite, goethite); nearly no anhydrite; no phyllosilicates; and considerably more pyroxene" — [Hausrath et al., LPSC 2026 #1489](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1489.pdf)
- The pie charts (Fig. 2, no numbers in the text) show NS containing plagioclase, K-spar, pyroxene, hematite, goethite, minor siderite, anhydrite, bassanite, crystalline Mg-sulfate hydrate, phyllosilicate and amorphous material — [Hausrath et al., LPSC 2026 #1489](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1489.pdf)
- "Preliminary CheMin and SAM analyses ... indicate that while both targets have a detrital component, the ridge is enriched in phyllosilicates, and the hollow in siderite ... an exciting result indicating different fluid histories" — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)
- JPL press release: "X-ray analysis found clay minerals in the ridge and carbonate minerals in the hollow" — [JPL, 23 Feb 2026](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-curiosity-rover-sees-martian-spiderwebs-up-close/)
- The first drilled sample in the Altadena member (Altadena) already contained phyllosilicates, which had been missing from the underlying Chenapau member (layered-sulfate unit) — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)
- The moles of Fe in siderite in VL are about equal to the moles of Fe in goethite + hematite in AD and NS. The authors read this as a change in oxidation state, "consistent with formation of the goethite and hematite from siderite" — [Hausrath et al., LPSC 2026 #1489](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1489.pdf)
- Lower pyroxene in AD and NS suggests more aqueous alteration (pyroxene dissolves faster at pH ~3-6.5). The clays in AD and NS could have formed by alteration of the original pyroxene, which fits the measured pyroxene and clay volumes — [Hausrath et al., LPSC 2026 #1489](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1489.pdf)
- More clay might make ridges *less* erosion-resistant, but Fe oxy(hydr)oxides could make them *more* resistant, by analogy with Vera Rubin ridge (hematite 2.9-14.7%) — [Hausrath et al., LPSC 2026 #1489](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1489.pdf)

**Chemistry (APXS, ChemCam)**
- "Neither of the remote sensing chemical instruments on the rover observe clear consistent chemical differences between the ridges and hollows." If groundwater alteration caused the difference in erosion resistance, it was probably isochemical (closed system) — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)
- APXS/ChemCam show "small and non-systematic differences" between ridges and hollows, and bulk compositions are similar — [Hausrath et al., LPSC 2026 #1489](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1489.pdf); the blog likewise reports "subtle differences ... but no striking ones" — [NASA blog, Sols 4695-4701 (Farrand)](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/curiosity-blog-sols-4695-4701-searching-for-answers-at-monte-grande)
- The Altadena member has relatively more Al and Si and less Mg and S than the Chenapau member below it (the orbital "layered sulfate unit", which holds crystalline Mg-sulfates) — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)
- The known chemical endmembers are light-toned veins and some nodules enriched in Ca-sulfate — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)
- The bedrock between ridges contains many tiny fractures filled with white calcium-sulfate veins, common lower on Mount Sharp but not seen in the sulfate layer until then — [JPL, 23 Jun 2025](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-curiosity-mars-rover-starts-unpacking-boxwork-formations/)

**Textures by position (diagenesis)**
- Ridge tops are fairly flat, often dust-covered, with dense light-toned veins. Ridge walls have veins and larger nodules near the top, with fewer veins and smaller nodules toward the base. Hollows show dense fine nodules, dendritic nodules and/or flaky textures — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)
- Pea-sized nodules, minerals left as groundwater dried, occur on ridge walls and in hollows, *not* near the central fractures. This is unexplained; one idea is that the ridges were cemented first and later groundwater episodes formed the nodules. Seeger: "We can't quite explain yet why the nodules appear where they do" — [JPL, 23 Feb 2026](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-curiosity-rover-sees-martian-spiderwebs-up-close/)
- In one hollow, cm-scale polygonal raised ridges with ~120° angles point to repeated expansion-contraction or dehydration — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)

**Ridge centrelines / fractures**
- On the ground, "the dark central lines appear to be accumulations of sand or regolith, and no imaging or approach angles have shown an evident fracture fill in the ridge centers," which fits open fractures — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)
- JPL says close-up observations confirmed the dark lines are fractures, supporting the 2014 "central fracture" hypothesis — [JPL, 23 Feb 2026](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-curiosity-rover-sees-martian-spiderwebs-up-close/)
- Fracture intersection angles (orbital mapping) are about orthogonal, the same across all 14 km of boxwork and similar to fractures in neighbouring units that have no resistant ridges, so they do not identify the fracturing mechanism — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)
- The team is also weighing a secondary hypothesis that the boxwork is purely erosional (tafoni-like), which the lack of chemical difference cannot yet rule out — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)

### Inferences
- A better one-line rule for the team: "ridges = clay + Fe-oxide (oxidised) cemented zones with Ca-sulfate veins; hollows = siderite-bearing (reduced, less altered, more pyroxene) bedrock; same bulk chemistry." Note the redox contrast, not just clay vs carbonate.
- The evidence rests on **one drill sample per setting** (n=1 ridge, n=1 hollow) plus the off-pair Altadena background. Treat the clay/carbonate split as a hypothesis-level, preliminary result.
- Clays at Altadena, outside the ridge/hollow pair, mean clays are a property of the Altadena member as a whole, more strongly expressed in ridges. They are not a unique ridge marker.
- Ridge centrelines have not shown a mineral fill. Their science value is as fluid conduits (structural), not as sites with confirmed different mineralogy.

### Gaps
- No numeric CheMin abundances were found in text form (only pie charts). The O'Connell-Cooper (chemistry), Seeger (textures), Willcocks (nodules) and Dietrich (formation) LPSC 2026 abstracts were cited but not read.
- No results found for the SAM TMAH (organics) run on Nevado Sajama 2, or for SAM evolved-gas data.
- No peer-reviewed in-situ boxwork paper found as of Nov 2026.

## 2. Why are the boxwork features scientifically important (groundwater, late water, habitability)?

### Takeaway
The boxwork records **groundwater flowing through fractures high on Mount Sharp, inside the sulfate-bearing interval that formed as Mars dried**. This implies a high water table persisting later than expected, which extends the habitability window. The ridges were proposed as targets for organic preservation.

### Cited Findings
- Siebach & Grotzinger (peer-reviewed) interpreted the ridges as early-diagenetic cements formed in the phreatic (saturated) zone, with groundwater at about −3,620 m elevation (~1,050 m above today's crater floor). They estimated at least 5.25×10⁵ m³ of cement, needing 800-6,700 pore volumes of water each, so a minimum of 0.43 km³ of water. They flagged the ridges as a Curiosity target because early mineralization could preserve a habitable environment — [Siebach & Grotzinger 2014, JGR Planets, doi:10.1002/2013JE004508 (Caltech record)](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/46110)
- "Seeing boxwork this far up the mountain suggests the groundwater table had to be pretty high" (Seeger). Water able to support life may have lasted longer than thought — [JPL, 23 Feb 2026](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-curiosity-rover-sees-martian-spiderwebs-up-close/)
- The boxwork lies in a layer rich in Mg-sulfates, which form as water dries up. This shows groundwater was still present underground as the climate grew drier. The team planned to search the cemented ridges for organic molecules. Vasavada: "A big mystery is why the ridges were hardened into these big patterns and why only here" — [JPL, 23 Jun 2025](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-curiosity-mars-rover-starts-unpacking-boxwork-formations/)
- No boxwork has been found anywhere else on Mount Sharp, from the rover or from orbit — [JPL, 23 Jun 2025](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-curiosity-mars-rover-starts-unpacking-boxwork-formations/)
- CheMin team: aqueous alteration in these younger sediments means "the period of martian habitability may have extended for much longer periods of time" — [Hausrath et al., LPSC 2026 #1489](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1489.pdf)
- Siderite to Fe-oxide conversion links to the Gale carbonate / carbon-cycle work cited by the CheMin team (Tutolo et al. 2025, *Science* 388:292-297) — [Hausrath et al., LPSC 2026 #1489](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1489.pdf)
- SAM's last TMAH (wet chemistry, organics) cup was used on a sample from beside the Nevado Sajama ridge hole. This was only the second TMAH run in the mission, the first almost six years earlier — [NASA blog, Sols 4788-4797 (via astrobiology.com)](https://astrobiology.com/2026/02/08/mars-curiosity-seeks-evidence-of-organics/); JPL notes a fourth sample analysed with wet chemistry to make organics easier to detect — [JPL, 23 Feb 2026](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-curiosity-rover-sees-martian-spiderwebs-up-close/)

### Inferences
- The mission chose the **ridge** for the last, scarce TMAH organics experiment. This is a strong signal that the team rates ridge material as the highest-value target for organic preservation.
- The hollow's siderite is scientifically valuable on its own (a carbonate, which is rare on Mars, plus a reduced-iron record and links to the carbon cycle). Hollows are not "low value". They record a different fluid history.

### Gaps
- No published outcome of the organics search.

## 3. Drill targets of the boxwork campaign (names, sols)

### Takeaway
Four boxwork-related drill holes: **Altadena** (background/transition, sol ~4564, Jun 2025), **Valle de la Luna** (hollow, 44th hole, ~late Oct 2025, sol ~4694), **Nevado Sajama** (ridge, Nov 2025, sol ~4711-4715) and **Nevado Sajama 2** (re-drill a few cm away for SAM TMAH, ~late Jan-early Feb 2026). After the boxwork, **Atacama** and **Campo Marte** were drilled in the layered-sulfate unit just above it (Apr-May 2026).

### Cited Findings
- Altadena: Mastcam image of the hole on 8 Jun 2025 = sol 4564. CheMin and SAM analysed it — [NASA blog, Sols 4568-4569](https://science.nasa.gov/blog/curiosity-blog-sols-4568-4569-a-close-look-at-the-altadena-drill-hole-and-tailings/); the drill date was 8 Jun — [JPL, 23 Jun 2025](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-curiosity-mars-rover-starts-unpacking-boxwork-formations/). Altadena is at the edge of the boxwork and was described as the "transitional area before the ridges" — [JPL, 23 Feb 2026](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-curiosity-rover-sees-martian-spiderwebs-up-close/)
- Valle de la Luna: bedrock in the Monte Grande hollow, drilled the weekend before the 24 Oct 2025 plan (sols 4695-4701), "44th hole on Mars" — [NASA blog, Sols 4695-4701](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/curiosity-blog-sols-4695-4701-searching-for-answers-at-monte-grande)
- Nevado Sajama: a ridge block about 10 m from Valle de la Luna and about 2 m higher. Drill sol 1 was in the sols 4709-4715 plan (7 Nov 2025). The team brushed it and ran APXS/ChemCam with comparison targets "Volcan Isluga", "Luna Muerte" and "Sipe Sipe" — [NASA blog, Sols 4709-4715 (O'Connell-Cooper)](https://science.nasa.gov/blog/curiosity-blog-sols-4709-4715-drilling-high-and-low-in-the-boxwork-unit/)
- Nevado Sajama 2: a new hole a few cm from the November hole, for SAM TMAH, planned after conjunction (30 Jan 2026) — [NASA blog, Sols 4788-4797 (via astrobiology.com)](https://astrobiology.com/2026/02/08/mars-curiosity-seeks-evidence-of-organics/); MAHLI imaged the hole on sol 4803 (9 Feb 2026) — [NASA blog, Sols 4804-4811](https://science.nasa.gov/blog/curiosity-blog-sols-4804-4811-kicking-off-the-final-phase-of-boxwork-exploration/)
- Phase 4 (sols 4805-4848) had no drilling. It covered APXS/MAHLI targets ("Toro Wharku", "Mollecita", "Monte Cielo"), ridge "Los Flamencos", and Mastcam mosaics of the southern boxwork / layered-sulfate contact. The rover was expected to cross the contact on sol 4844 — [NASA blog, Sols 4838-4844 (Padgett, 26 Mar 2026)](https://science.nasa.gov/blog/curiosity-blog-sols-4838-4844-wrapping-up-the-boxwork-terrain/); first workspace across the contact was "Santa Rosa" (layered-sulfate bedrock), around sol 4848 (27 Mar 2026) — [Sols 4845-4851 (astrobiology.com mirror)](https://astrobiology.com/2026/04/20/mars-curiosity-blog-sols-4845-4851-bye-bye-boxwork-bye-bye/)
- Post-boxwork: Atacama was the first drill in layered-sulfate bedrock above the boxwork (blog sols 4873-4878), Campo Marte was in the same stratum, and Nazca was the 49th hole (sol ~5050, Oct 2026) — [NASA blog, Sols 4873-4878](https://science.nasa.gov/blog/curiosity-blog-sols-4873-4878-welcome-to-the-atacama-drill-target/)

### Inferences
- The numbering fits Altadena = 43, Valle de la Luna = 44, Nevado Sajama = 45, Nevado Sajama 2 = 46, Atacama = 47, Campo Marte = 48, Nazca = 49. This is inferred and only #44 and #49 are confirmed.
- The ridge-hollow pair is about 10 m apart horizontally and about 2 m vertically. This is a useful scale for a "ridge vs hollow" station pair.
- Exact sols for the Valle de la Luna and Nevado Sajama holes are approximate (from blog planning windows).
- The planned "additional drill site" (Hausrath abstract) seems to have been Nevado Sajama 2 (SAM-only), not a new CheMin site in the boxwork. Unconfirmed.

### Gaps
- The exact drilling sols for Valle de la Luna and Nevado Sajama were not stated in the sources read.

## 4. Hazards in the boxwork (sand in hollows, wheel slip)

### Takeaway
**Sandy hollows (wheel slip, hard to find bedrock), narrow ridges barely wider than the rover**, plus the routine rock-coherency check before drilling.

### Cited Findings
- Ridges stand 1-2 m tall with sandy hollows between them. Curiosity (~900 kg) must cross ridges barely wider than itself. Stroupe (JPL): "we have to go down into the hollows, where you need to be mindful of Curiosity's wheels slipping" — [JPL, 23 Feb 2026](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-curiosity-rover-sees-martian-spiderwebs-up-close/)
- Hollow floors are often covered by sand and pebbles, which makes bedrock hard to find for drilling. Rover planners test rock coherency before drilling — [NASA blog, Sols 4709-4715](https://science.nasa.gov/blog/curiosity-blog-sols-4709-4715-drilling-high-and-low-in-the-boxwork-unit/)
- Polygons are largest near the top of the unit, where hollows tend to be sand-filled — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)
- One plan noted that the rover was not on stable enough ground to brush, with dusty bedrock at the contact workspace — [Sols 4845-4851 (astrobiology.com mirror)](https://astrobiology.com/2026/04/20/mars-curiosity-blog-sols-4845-4851-bye-bye-boxwork-bye-bye/)

### Inferences
- For station costing: hollow stations carry a traversability penalty (sand, slip) and a lower chance of exposed bedrock. Ridge stations carry the risk of narrow crests and edge exposure.

### Gaps
- No quantitative slip values or wheel-damage reports specific to the boxwork were found.

## 5. Orbital identification and pre-arrival hypotheses

### Takeaway
The boxwork was spotted from orbit (~2008) and mapped with HiRISE imagery and a DTM. It is a polygonal ridge network spanning ~14 km and up to ~100 m of elevation. The pre-arrival hypothesis was **cemented fracture halos from groundwater, then wind erosion of the weaker hollows**. In-situ data keep this overall model but leave the strengthening mechanism unclear.

### Cited Findings
- Orbital definition: erosion-resistant ridges about 5 m wide, forming polygons about 10-20 m across and 1-2 m deep. The unit is about 1 km above the crater floor, with exposures extending 14 km west of the traverse over up to 100 m of elevation. Polygons are largest near the top, where some ridges show a dark central line — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)
- Orbital hypothesis: strengthened zones ("fracture halos/skins") around groundwater-conduit fractures, with fluid percolating about 3 m into the rock on each side and then eolian erosion. The five steps are deposition, fracturing, fluid flow, cementation/alteration and eolian erosion — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)
- Siebach & Grotzinger mapped "light-toned, polygonal, parallel-sided ridges with dark central linear depressions" from high-resolution orbital imagery and a DTM — [Caltech record of Siebach & Grotzinger 2014](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/46110)
- Orbiters first spotted the boxwork in 2008 and HiRISE later imaged it — [National Geographic, 2014](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/140307-mars-water-habitable-boxwork-gale-crater-space)
- On the ground, the host rock is fine-grained, planar-laminated, darker bedrock interpreted as lacustrine. It is named the **Altadena member of the Mirador formation**, distinct from the cross-bedded eolian Chenapau member below. One outcrop (Volcán Peña Blanca) is fluvial — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)
- Summary: the overall hypothesis "is still relevant, although the mechanism by which ridges were strengthened is less clear. There are differences in diagenetic textures and mineralogy between ridges and hollows, but not chemistry" — [Siebach et al., LPSC 2026 #1716](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1716.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Contacts** (Chenapau/Altadena at entry, Altadena/layered-sulfate at the southern exit) were explicit Phase 4 targets. Their value comes from constraining the timing and extent of the inferred lake and the end of the boxwork interval. Clays appear in Altadena but not in Chenapau. These facts support giving contact stations high science value.

### Gaps
- No CRISM spectral identification specific to the boxwork was found. Orbital identification in the sources read is morphological (HiRISE/DTM). The underlying unit is mapped from orbit as part of the layered-sulfate sequence.
- The full text of Siebach & Grotzinger 2014 was not read, so the HiRISE image IDs and mapped area are not confirmed.
